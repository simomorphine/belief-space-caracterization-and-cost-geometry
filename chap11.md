# Chapter 10: Geometry of the γ-Deformation

---

In Chapter 9, we introduced the $\gamma$-family $d_\gamma = \sqrt{d^2 + \gamma^2 D^2}$ and proved that it interpolates continuously between the pure cost $d$ (at $\gamma = 0$) and the full modulus $|Q|$ (at $\gamma = 1$). We showed that each $d_\gamma$ is a quasi-metric, that the family is monotone in $\gamma$, and that it admits a clean norm-space interpretation.

This chapter develops the *geometry* of the deformation. We study how the balls, topologies, geodesics, and optimal paths change as $\gamma$ varies. The central theme is that the $\gamma$-family is not just a family of metrics—it is a *deformation of the geometry of belief space*, and this deformation has structure.

The chapter culminates in the notion of a **$\gamma$-phase transition**: a value of $\gamma$ at which the geometry changes discontinuously.

---

## 10.1 The $\gamma$-balls

We begin with the balls.

**Definition 10.1 ($\gamma$-ball).** The *$\gamma$-ball of radius $r$ centered at $b$* is

$$B_\gamma(b, r) := \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d_\gamma(b, b') < r\}.$$

**Definition 10.2 (Forward and backward $\gamma$-balls).** The *forward $\gamma$-ball* is

$$B_\gamma^+(b, r) := \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d_\gamma(b, b') < r\},$$

and the *backward $\gamma$-ball* is

$$B_\gamma^-(b, r) := \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d_\gamma(b', b) < r\}.$$

**Proposition 10.3 (Nesting).** For $\gamma \le \gamma'$ and fixed $r > 0$,

$$B_{\gamma'}(b, r) \subseteq B_\gamma(b, r), \qquad B_{\gamma'}^+(b, r) \subseteq B_\gamma^+(b, r), \qquad B_{\gamma'}^-(b, r) \subseteq B_\gamma^-(b, r).$$

*Proof.* $d_\gamma \le d_{\gamma'}$ pointwise (Proposition 9.6). $\square$

**Remark 10.4.** Increasing $\gamma$ shrinks the balls. This is because increasing $\gamma$ increases distances, so fewer points are within distance $r$.

**Proposition 10.5 (Strict nesting).** If $D(b, b') \neq 0$ and $r > d_\gamma(b, b')$, then there exists $\gamma' > \gamma$ such that $b' \notin B_{\gamma'}(b, r)$.

*Proof.* $d_{\gamma'}(b, b')$ is strictly increasing in $\gamma'$ (Proposition 9.8), so for large enough $\gamma'$, $d_{\gamma'}(b, b') > r$. $\square$

**Remark 10.6.** The strict nesting says that points at nonzero debt eventually leave every ball as $\gamma$ increases. The rate at which they leave depends on the phase: points with large $|D|$ leave quickly; points with small $|D|$ leave slowly.

**Proposition 10.7 (Continuity of balls).** For fixed $b, r$, the set $B_\gamma(b, r)$ is continuous in $\gamma$ in the sense that

$$\lim_{\gamma' \to \gamma} B_{\gamma'}(b, r) = B_\gamma(b, r)$$

in the Hausdorff metric (assuming $\mathcal{B}$ is compact).

*Proof.* $d_\gamma$ is continuous in $\gamma$, so the sublevel sets $B_\gamma(b, r) = \{b' : d_\gamma(b, b') < r\}$ vary continuously. $\square$

**Remark 10.8.** The continuity of balls means that the geometry of the $\gamma$-family varies continuously with $\gamma$, at least at the level of balls. Topological changes (Chapter 10.3) can still occur abruptly.

## 10.2 The $\gamma$-topology

We now study the topology induced by $d_\gamma$.

**Definition 10.9 ($\gamma$-topology).** The *$\gamma$-topology* $\tau_\gamma$ is the topology on $\mathcal{B}$ generated by the $\gamma$-balls $\{B_\gamma(b, r) : b \in \mathcal{B}, r > 0\}$.

**Proposition 10.10 (Monotonicity).** For $\gamma \le \gamma'$, $\tau_{\gamma'} \subseteq \tau_\gamma$.

*Proof.* The balls $B_{\gamma'}(b, r)$ are subsets of $B_\gamma(b, r)$. So a set that is open in $\tau_{\gamma'}$ (a union of $\gamma'$-balls) is also open in $\tau_\gamma$ (a union of $\gamma$-balls). $\square$

**Remark 10.11.** Larger $\gamma$ gives a coarser topology. The finest topology is $\tau_0$ (the topology of $d$); the coarsest is $\tau_1$ (the topology of $|Q|$).

**Proposition 10.12 (Equality of topologies).** If $d$ and $|Q|$ induce the same topology, then all $\tau_\gamma$ coincide.

*Proof.* If $\tau_0 = \tau_1$, then by monotonicity $\tau_0 \supseteq \tau_\gamma \supseteq \tau_1$ for all $\gamma \in [0, 1]$, so $\tau_\gamma = \tau_0 = \tau_1$. $\square$

**Remark 10.13.** The $\gamma$-topologies are not necessarily distinct. In many cases, they coincide. The interesting cases are those in which they differ.

**Definition 10.14 (Critical $\gamma$).** A value $\gamma_c \in [0, 1]$ is *critical* if $\tau_\gamma$ changes at $\gamma_c$, i.e., if there exist $\gamma_1 < \gamma_c < \gamma_2$ with $\tau_{\gamma_1} \neq \tau_{\gamma_2}$.

**Proposition 10.15 (Finitely many critical values).** If $\mathcal{B}$ is finite, then there are finitely many critical values of $\gamma$.

*Proof.* The topology $\tau_\gamma$ is determined by the balls, which are determined by the values $d_\gamma(b, b')$ for $b, b' \in \mathcal{B}$. These are continuous functions of $\gamma$, and the topology changes only when the order of these values changes (or when a ball crosses a threshold). There are finitely many such events. $\square$

**Remark 10.16.** For infinite $\mathcal{B}$, there may be infinitely many critical values, or a continuous range of them. The analysis is more delicate.

## 10.3 $\gamma$-phase transitions

We now introduce the notion of a $\gamma$-phase transition.

**Definition 10.17 (Phase transition).** A *$\gamma$-phase transition* is a value $\gamma_c \in [0, 1]$ at which the topology $\tau_\gamma$ changes discontinuously.

**Proposition 10.18 (Characterization).** A value $\gamma_c$ is a phase transition if and only if there exists a pair $(b, b')$ such that the set of $r > 0$ with $b' \in B_\gamma(b, r)$ changes discontinuously at $\gamma_c$.

*Proof.* The topology is determined by the balls; if the balls change discontinuously, so does the topology. $\square$

**Proposition 10.19 (Existence of transitions).** If $d$ and $|Q|$ induce different topologies, then there exists at least one phase transition.

*Proof.* $\tau_0 \supsetneq \tau_1$, and the map $\gamma \mapsto \tau_\gamma$ is monotone. By the intermediate value theorem for topologies (in the appropriate sense), there must be a $\gamma_c$ at which the topology changes. $\square$

**Remark 10.20.** Phase transitions are the discontinuous changes in the geometry of belief space as $\gamma$ varies. They are the "critical points" of the deformation.

**Example 10.21 (Two-point space).** Let $\mathcal{B} = \{1, 2\}$ with $d(1, 2) = 1$, $d(2, 1) = 1$, $D(1, 2) = 1$. Then $d_\gamma(1, 2) = \sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}$. The topology is discrete for all $\gamma$ (since $\mathcal{B}$ is finite), so there are no phase transitions.

**Example 10.22 (Three-point space with collapsing balls).** Let $\mathcal{B} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ with

$$d = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then $d_\gamma(1, 3) = \sqrt{1 + \gamma^2}$, $d_\gamma(1, 2) = 1$, $d_\gamma(2, 3) = 1$. The ball $B_\gamma(1, 1.5)$ contains $2$ for all $\gamma$, but contains $3$ only if $\sqrt{1 + \gamma^2} < 1.5$, i.e., $\gamma < \sqrt{1.25} \approx 1.118$. Since $\gamma \in [0, 1]$, the ball contains $3$ for all $\gamma \in [0, 1]$. So no phase transition in this example.

Let me construct a proper example.

**Example 10.23 (Phase transition).** Let $\mathcal{B} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ with

$$d = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -3 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Then:

- $d_\gamma(1, 2) = 1$, $d_\gamma(2, 3) = 1$, $d_\gamma(1, 3) = \sqrt{4 + 9\gamma^2}$.
- For $\gamma = 0$: $d_0(1, 3) = 2$. The ball $B_0(1, 2.5)$ contains $2$ and $3$.
- For $\gamma = 1$: $d_1(1, 3) = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.606$. The ball $B_1(1, 2.5)$ contains $2$ but not $3$.

The critical value is $\gamma_c$ such that $\sqrt{4 + 9\gamma_c^2} = 2.5$, i.e., $4 + 9\gamma_c^2 = 6.25$, i.e., $\gamma_c^2 = 0.25$, i.e., $\gamma_c = 0.5$.

At $\gamma_c = 0.5$, the point $3$ leaves the ball $B_\gamma(1, 2.5)$. This is a phase transition: the topology changes at $\gamma_c$.

**Remark 10.24.** The example shows that phase transitions can occur at non-trivial values of $\gamma$. The critical value depends on the specific geometry of $d$ and $D$.

**Proposition 10.25 (Phase transitions are generic).** For a generic belief space, phase transitions occur at isolated values of $\gamma$.

*Proof.* The topology changes when a ball crosses a threshold, which is a codimension-1 event in the space of parameters. Generic parameters avoid these events except at isolated values. $\square$

**Remark 10.26.** The genericity statement is informal. A precise statement would require specifying a measure on the space of belief spaces and showing that the set of $\gamma$ with phase transitions has measure zero. This is beyond the scope of this book.

## 10.4 Geodesics and optimal paths

We now turn to geodesics.

**Definition 10.27 ($\gamma$-geodesic).** A path $\gamma = (b_0, b_1, \dots, b_n)$ is a *$\gamma$-geodesic* if it minimizes the total $\gamma$-distance:

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} d_\gamma(b_k, b_{k+1}) = d_\gamma(b_0, b_n).$$

**Proposition 10.28.** A path is a $\gamma$-geodesic if and only if each intermediate step is optimal in the $\gamma$-metric.

*Proof.* By the triangle inequality for $d_\gamma$, the total distance is at least $d_\gamma(b_0, b_n)$, with equality iff the path is optimal. $\square$

**Definition 10.29 ($\gamma$-shortest path).** The *$\gamma$-shortest path* between $b_i$ and $b_j$ is any path that achieves the minimum of $\sum d_\gamma$ over all paths from $b_i$ to $b_j$.

**Proposition 10.30 (Dependence on $\gamma$).** The set of $\gamma$-shortest paths depends on $\gamma$ in general.

*Proof.* The $\gamma$-distance depends on $\gamma$, so the optimal path may change as $\gamma$ varies. $\square$

**Example 10.31 (Path change).** Consider a belief space with three points $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and two paths from $1$ to $3$:

- Path A: $1 \to 3$ directly, with $d(1, 3) = 5$, $D(1, 3) = 0$.
- Path B: $1 \to 2 \to 3$, with $d(1, 2) = 2$, $D(1, 2) = 2$, $d(2, 3) = 2$, $D(2, 3) = 0$.

At $\gamma = 0$: Path A costs $5$; Path B costs $2 + 2 = 4$. Path B is shorter.

At $\gamma = 1$: Path A costs $5$; Path B costs $\sqrt{4 + 4} + 2 = \sqrt{8} + 2 \approx 4.828$. Path B is still shorter.

**Example 10.32 (Path change, corrected).** Consider three points $\{1, 2, 3\}$ with:

- Path A: $1 \to 3$ directly, with $d(1, 3) = 5$, $D(1, 3) = 0$.
- Path B: $1 \to 2 \to 3$, with $d(1, 2) = 2$, $D(1, 2) = 3$, $d(2, 3) = 2$, $D(2, 3) = 0$.

At $\gamma = 0$: Path A costs $5$; Path B costs $2 + 2 = 4$. Path B is shorter.

The critical value is $\gamma_c$ such that $5 = \sqrt{4 + 9\gamma_c^2} + 2$, i.e., $\sqrt{4 + 9\gamma_c^2} = 3$, i.e., $4 + 9\gamma_c^2 = 9$, i.e., $\gamma_c^2 = 5/9$, i.e., $\gamma_c = \sqrt{5}/3 \approx 0.745$.

For $\gamma < \gamma_c$, Path B is shorter; for $\gamma > \gamma_c$, Path A is shorter. This is a path change at $\gamma_c$.

**Remark 10.33.** The example shows that the optimal path can change as $\gamma$ varies. This is a "geodesic transition," analogous to a phase transition but for optimal paths rather than topologies.

**Proposition 10.34 (Finitely many path changes).** If $\mathcal{B}$ is finite, then the set of $\gamma$ at which the optimal path changes is finite.

*Proof.* The optimal path is determined by the comparison of finitely many path costs, each a continuous function of $\gamma$. The minimum changes only when two path costs cross, which happens at finitely many $\gamma$. $\square$

**Remark 10.35.** For infinite $\mathcal{B}$, there may be infinitely many path changes. The analysis is more delicate.

## 10.5 The $\gamma$-diameter

We now study the diameter of belief space in the $\gamma$-metric.

**Definition 10.36 ($\gamma$-diameter).** The *$\gamma$-diameter* of $\mathcal{B}$ is

$$\mathrm{diam}_\gamma(\mathcal{B}) := \sup_{b_i, b_j \in \mathcal{B}} d_\gamma(b_i, b_j).$$

**Proposition 10.37 (Monotonicity of diameter).** $\mathrm{diam}_\gamma(\mathcal{B})$ is non-decreasing in $\gamma$.

*Proof.* $d_\gamma \le d_{\gamma'}$ for $\gamma \le \gamma'$, so the supremum is non-decreasing. $\square$

**Proposition 10.38 (Diameter in terms of cost and debt).**

$$\mathrm{diam}_\gamma(\mathcal{B}) = \sup_{b_i, b_j} \sqrt{d(b_i, b_j)^2 + \gamma^2 D(b_i, b_j)^2}.$$

**Example 10.39.** If $\mathcal{B}$ is finite, the diameter is finite for all $\gamma$. If $\mathcal{B}$ is infinite, the diameter may be infinite.

**Remark 10.40.** The diameter is a crude measure of the "size" of belief space. It is monotone in $\gamma$, increasing as debt is weighted more heavily.

## 10.6 The $\gamma$-curvature

We now introduce the notion of $\gamma$-curvature, which measures the failure of the triangle inequality to be sharp.

**Definition 10.41 ($\gamma$-curvature).** The *$\gamma$-curvature* of a triple $(b_i, b_j, b_k)$ is

$$\kappa_\gamma(b_i, b_j, b_k) := d_\gamma(b_i, b_j) + d_\gamma(b_j, b_k) - d_\gamma(b_i, b_k).$$

**Proposition 10.42.** $\kappa_\gamma \ge 0$ for all triples.

*Proof.* Triangle inequality. $\square$

**Proposition 10.43.** $\kappa_\gamma = 0$ if and only if the path through $b_j$ is a $\gamma$-geodesic.

*Proof.* Definition of geodesic. $\square$

**Remark 10.44.** The $\gamma$-curvature measures the "excess" of the path through $b_j$ over the direct transition. It is non-negative, and zero iff the path is geodesic.

**Proposition 10.45 (Monotonicity of curvature).** $\kappa_\gamma$ is not necessarily monotone in $\gamma$.

*Proof.* The $\gamma$-distances are individually monotone, but their combination in the curvature is not. $\square$

**Remark 10.46.** The non-monotonicity of curvature is a subtle point. It means that the geometry can become more or less "curved" as $\gamma$ varies, depending on the specific triple.

## 10.7 The $\gamma$-deformation as a homotopy

We now interpret the $\gamma$-deformation as a homotopy of metric structures.

**Definition 10.47 (Metric homotopy).** A *metric homotopy* on $\mathcal{B}$ is a continuous map $\gamma \mapsto d_\gamma$ from $[0, 1]$ to the space of quasi-metrics on $\mathcal{B}$ (with the topology of pointwise convergence).

**Proposition 10.48.** The $\gamma$-family is a metric homotopy.

*Proof.* $d_\gamma$ is continuous in $\gamma$ (Proposition 9.24). $\square$

**Proposition 10.49 (Homotopy of balls).** The balls $B_\gamma(b, r)$ vary continuously in $\gamma$ in the Hausdorff metric (assuming $\mathcal{B}$ is compact).

*Proof.* Proposition 10.7. $\square$

**Remark 10.50.** The $\gamma$-family is a homotopy of metric structures, but not a homotopy of *topologies*. The topology can change discontinuously (phase transitions), even though the metric varies continuously.

**Remark 10.51.** The distinction between metric homotopy and topological homotopy is important. The metric varies smoothly, but the topology can jump. This is the origin of phase transitions.

---

## 10.8 The $\gamma$-family and the basepoint

We close this section by noting the basepoint independence of the $\gamma$-geometry.

**Proposition 10.52.** All $\gamma$-geometric quantities—balls, topologies, geodesics, diameters, curvatures—are independent of the choice of basepoint.

*Proof.* They depend only on $d_\gamma$, which is basepoint-independent (Proposition 9.46). $\square$

**Remark 10.53.** The basepoint independence is a manifestation of the gauge invariance of the theory. The $\gamma$-geometry is an observable, independent of the choice of potential.

## 10.10 The $\gamma$-deformation: summary

We summarize the $\gamma$-geometry in a single table.

| **Quantity** | **Definition** | **Dependence on $\gamma$** |
|---|---|---|
| $\gamma$-ball | $B_\gamma(b, r)$ | Nested, shrinking |
| $\gamma$-topology | $\tau_\gamma$ | Monotone coarsening |
| $\gamma$-geodesic | Minimizer of $\sum d_\gamma$ | Changes at critical $\gamma$ |
| $\gamma$-diameter | $\sup d_\gamma$ | Non-decreasing |
| $\gamma$-curvature | $\kappa_\gamma$ | Not monotone |
| Phase transition | Topology change | Isolated values |
| Path change | Optimal path change | Isolated values |

**Key results:**

- **Nesting.** $B_{\gamma'} \subseteq B_\gamma$ for $\gamma \le \gamma'$.
- **Topology monotonicity.** $\tau_{\gamma'} \subseteq \tau_\gamma$ for $\gamma \le \gamma'$.
- **Phase transitions.** Isolated values $\gamma_c$ at which the topology changes.
- **Path changes.** Isolated values $\gamma_c$ at which the optimal path changes.
- **Metric homotopy.** $d_\gamma$ is a continuous deformation of $d$.
- **Topological non-homotopy.** The topology can change discontinuously.

The $\gamma$-geometry is the study of how the geometry of belief space depends on the parameter $\gamma$. It is the bridge between the complex quasi-metric (Chapter 6) and the polar decomposition and topology lattice (Part V).
