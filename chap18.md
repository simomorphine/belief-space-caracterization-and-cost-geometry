# Chapter 17: On the Nature of Cost

---

We have now completed the mathematical development of the theory. Parts I–VI built the complex quasi-metric from its foundations, and Chapter 16 surveyed the open structure. What remains is a reflection: what does the theory *mean*?

This chapter is philosophical, but not in a vague or speculative sense. It addresses a precise question: **what is the ontological status of cost and debt?** Are they features of the world, or features of our descriptions of the world? Are they discovered or imposed?

The answer, developed in this chapter, is that cost and debt admit two distinct interpretations, corresponding to two distinct stances:

- The **designer stance**: cost and debt are features of a system we have built. We choose the axioms; we impose the structure; we are responsible for the consequences.
- The **describer stance**: cost and debt are features of a system we are observing. We discover the structure; we infer the axioms; we are humble about what we claim.

The two stances give different vocabularies for the same mathematical structure. The designer says "tolerance." The describer says "potential difference." Both are correct; they are answers to different questions.

This chapter develops the two stances, their relationship, and their implications.

---

## 17.1 The designer stance

We begin with the designer stance.

**The designer.** A *designer* is someone who *builds* an information processing system. They choose the states, the transitions, the cost function. They impose the axioms. They are responsible for the structure.

**The designer's vocabulary.**

- **Cost.** The resource required to perform a transition. The designer chooses what counts as cost and how to measure it.
- **Debt.** The correction term that emerges when the cost fails to be a metric. The designer *tolerates* a certain amount of non-metricity, and the tolerance is expressed as debt.
- **Tolerance.** The amount of non-metricity the designer is willing to accept. Tolerance is a design parameter: the designer can set it high (accepting lots of non-metricity) or low (insisting on near-metricity).
- **Constraint.** The structural requirements the designer imposes: additivity, antisymmetry, gauge invariance, etc. The designer can impose them or not.

**The designer's stance.** The designer says: "I am building this system. I choose the cost function. I impose the axioms. The debt is what I tolerate when the cost is not a metric. The structure is mine."

**The designer's responsibility.** The designer is responsible for the consequences of their choices. If the cost function is asymmetric, the designer must accept the resulting bitopological structure. If the debt is not exact, the designer must accept the resulting holonomy. The designer cannot blame the structure on the world; the structure is their creation.

**Remark 17.1.** The designer stance is the stance of engineering. It is the stance of someone who builds a system and is accountable for its behavior. It is a stance of *authority*: the designer has the authority to impose the axioms, and the responsibility to accept the consequences.

**Remark 17.2.** The designer stance is not the only stance. It is one of two. The other is the describer stance.

---

## 17.2 The describer stance

We now turn to the describer stance.

**The describer.** A *describer* is someone who *observes* an information processing system. They do not build it; they encounter it. They infer the structure from observations. They are humble about what they claim.

**The describer's vocabulary.**

- **Cost.** The observed resource expenditure for a transition. The describer measures it; they do not choose it.
- **Debt.** The observed potential difference. The describer names it, but does not claim to know what it is.
- **Potential difference.** The difference in some underlying potential between two states. The describer observes the difference, not the potential itself.
- **Humility.** The recognition that the describer does not know the underlying structure. They only observe differences, not absolutes.

**The describer's stance.** The describer says: "I am observing this system. I measure the cost. I infer the axioms. The debt is a potential difference I observe, but I do not claim to know what the potential is. The structure is discovered, not imposed."

**The describer's humility.** The describer is humble about what they claim. They do not say "the cost *is* a metric"; they say "the cost *behaves as if* it were a metric in certain regimes." They do not say "the debt *is* a potential difference"; they say "the debt *is observable as* a potential difference." The describer names the pattern, not the substance.

**Remark 17.3.** The describer stance is the stance of science. It is the stance of someone who encounters a system and tries to understand it. It is a stance of *humility*: the describer does not claim to know the underlying structure, only the observable differences.

**Remark 17.4.** The describer stance is not the only stance. It is one of two. The other is the designer stance.

---

## 17.3 The two stances compared

We now compare the two stances.

| **Aspect** | **Designer** | **Describer** |
|---|---|---|
| Stance | Stipulative | Descriptive |
| Relationship | Builds the system | Observes the system |
| Authority | Imposes the axioms | Infers the axioms |
| Vocabulary | Tolerance | Potential difference |
| Responsibility | Accepts consequences | Names the pattern |
| Humility | Low (authority) | High (humility) |
| Failure mode | Over-constraining | Under-explaining |
| Correct when | You built it | You found it |

**Remark 17.5.** The two stances are not contradictory. They are answers to different questions. The designer asks "what shall I build?" The describer asks "what have I found?" The same mathematical structure can be described from both stances, with different vocabularies.

**Remark 17.6.** The two stances are related by a *translation*. The designer's "tolerance" is the describer's "potential difference." The designer's "constraint" is the describer's "axiom." The translation is not perfect (the stances have different emphases), but it is systematic.

**Remark 17.7.** The choice of stance is not a matter of truth. It is a matter of *perspective*. The same system can be viewed from either stance. The choice depends on the relationship between the observer and the system.

## 17.4 The debt: tolerance or potential difference?

We now apply the two stances to the debt.

**The debt from the designer stance.** The debt is the *tolerance* of the designer for non-metricity. The designer builds a cost function that is almost a metric, and the debt is the "slack" they allow. The designer can set the debt high (accepting lots of non-metricity) or low (insisting on near-metricity). The debt is a design parameter.

**The debt from the describer stance.** The debt is the *potential difference* between two states. The describer observes that transitions have a directional asymmetry, and they name the asymmetry "debt." The debt is a potential difference: it is the difference in some underlying potential between the two states. The describer does not claim to know what the potential is; they only observe the difference.

**The relationship.** The two interpretations are not contradictory. They are two ways of describing the same mathematical object. The designer's "tolerance" and the describer's "potential difference" are the same function $D(b_i, b_j) = \psi(b_j) - \psi(b_i)$, viewed from two different perspectives.

**Remark 17.8.** The designer's "tolerance" is a *choice*: the designer chooses how much non-metricity to accept. The describer's "potential difference" is an *observation*: the describer observes the potential difference, but does not choose it. The same function is a choice from one stance and an observation from the other.

**Remark 17.9.** The two stances give different *feelings* about the debt. From the designer stance, the debt is a concession: the designer would prefer a metric, but tolerates the debt. From the describer stance, the debt is a discovery: the describer finds a potential difference, and names it. The designer apologizes for the debt; the describer celebrates it.

**Remark 17.10.** The choice of stance matters for *what questions you ask*. The designer asks "how much debt should I tolerate?" The describer asks "what is the potential that generates this debt?" The same mathematical structure answers both questions, but the questions are different.

## 17.5 Gauge invariance and the two stances

We now apply the two stances to gauge invariance.

**Gauge invariance from the designer stance.** The designer chooses the potential $\psi$, but observes that the debt $D = \delta\psi$ is invariant under the addition of a constant to $\psi$. The designer recognizes that the constant is *gauge freedom*: it does not affect the observable structure. The designer can choose the gauge for convenience.

**Gauge invariance from the describer stance.** The describer observes that the debt $D$ is a potential difference, and that the potential itself is not observable. The describer recognizes that the potential is *defined only up to a constant*: only differences are observable. The describer is humble about the absolute value of the potential.

**The relationship.** The two interpretations are again two ways of describing the same mathematical fact. The designer's "gauge freedom" and the describer's "unobservability of the absolute potential" are the same statement: the potential is defined only up to a constant.

**Remark 17.11.** The designer's "gauge freedom" is a *convenience*: the designer chooses a gauge for computational ease. The describer's "unobservability" is an *epistemic fact*: the describer cannot observe the absolute potential. The same mathematical fact is a convenience from one stance and a limitation from the other.

**Remark 17.12.** The two stances give different *attitudes* toward gauge invariance. From the designer stance, gauge invariance is a feature to be exploited: the designer chooses the most convenient gauge. From the describer stance, gauge invariance is a limitation to be respected: the describer does not claim to know the absolute potential.

## 17.6 The basepoint theorem and the two stances

We now apply the two stances to the basepoint theorem.

**The basepoint theorem from the designer stance.** The designer builds a debt function that satisfies additivity and antisymmetry. The basepoint theorem says that *any* such debt function is a potential difference. The designer is not surprised: they imposed the axioms, and the theorem is a consequence. The basepoint theorem is a *design constraint*: if you want a debt function, you must accept that it is a potential difference.

**The basepoint theorem from the describer stance.** The describer observes a debt function and infers additivity and antisymmetry. The basepoint theorem says that the debt is a potential difference. The describer is surprised: they did not impose the axioms, yet the structure emerged. The basepoint theorem is a *discovery*: the debt is a potential difference, whether we like it or not.

**The relationship.** The basepoint theorem is the same theorem in both stances. What differs is the *attitude*: the designer sees it as a consequence of their choices, the describer sees it as a discovery about the world.

**Remark 17.13.** The basepoint theorem is the central result of the theory, and it is the point at which the two stances most clearly diverge. For the designer, the theorem is *trivial*: they imposed the axioms, and the theorem is a consequence. For the describer, the theorem is *profound*: they did not impose the axioms, yet the structure emerged.

**Remark 17.14.** This divergence is a general feature of mathematical theories. From the designer stance, a theorem is a consequence of axioms; from the describer stance, a theorem is a discovery about the world. The same theorem is both, depending on the stance.

## 17.7 Why cost was under-theorized

We now ask: why was cost under-theorized before this book?

**The metric assumption.** The standard assumption in information processing is that cost is a metric. This assumption is so pervasive that it is rarely stated. It is absorbed into the language: "the cost of moving from $A$ to $B$" is treated as a single, well-defined number, symmetric and satisfying the triangle inequality.

**The consequences of the assumption.** Because cost was assumed to be a metric, there was no need for a theory of non-metric cost. The metric theory was sufficient. The asymmetry, non-triangularity, path-dependence, and non-identity of cost were treated as defects, not as features.

**The oversight.** The oversight was to confuse the *designer stance* with the *describer stance*. From the designer stance, it is legitimate to impose the metric assumption: the designer can choose a metric cost function. From the describer stance, it is *not* legitimate: the describer observes whatever cost function the system has, and it may not be a metric.

**The correction.** This book corrects the oversight by developing the theory of non-metric cost. It shows that non-metricity is not a defect, but a feature: it has structure (the debt), and the structure is rich (the complex quasi-metric, the gauge group, the $\gamma$-family, the topology lattice, the $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading).

**Remark 17.15.** The under-theorization of cost is a case study in the danger of the designer stance. The designer imposes the metric assumption for convenience, and the describer inherits the assumption without question. The result is a theory that is precise but not general: it applies only to the systems the designer built, not to the systems the describer encounters.

**Remark 17.16.** The correction is to *distinguish the stances*. The designer may impose the metric assumption; the describer must not. The two stances give different theories: the designer's metric theory and the describer's complex quasi-metric theory. Both are valid; they answer different questions.

## 17.8 The role of mathematics

We now ask: what is the role of mathematics in the theory of cost?

**Mathematics as description.** From the describer stance, mathematics is a *language* for describing the observed structure. The complex quasi-metric is a mathematical object that captures the observed structure of cost and debt. The theorems are statements about the observed structure.

**Mathematics as construction.** From the designer stance, mathematics is a *tool* for constructing the desired structure. The complex quasi-metric is a mathematical object that the designer builds. The theorems are constraints on what can be built.

**The relationship.** The two roles are not contradictory. Mathematics is both a language and a tool. It describes what is, and it constructs what could be. The same theorem can be a description (from the describer stance) or a constraint (from the designer stance).

**Remark 17.17.** The dual role of mathematics is a general feature of mathematical theories. In physics, mathematics describes the world (from the describer stance) and constructs models (from the designer stance). In engineering, mathematics constructs systems (from the designer stance) and describes their behavior (from the describer stance). The same mathematics plays both roles.

**Remark 17.18.** The complex quasi-metric is a mathematical object with both roles. It describes the observed structure of cost and debt (describer stance), and it constructs a model of cost and debt (designer stance). The two roles are complementary.

## 17.9 The ethics of the two stances

We now address the ethical dimension of the two stances.

**The designer's responsibility.** The designer builds the system and is responsible for its behavior. If the system has asymmetric costs, the designer is responsible for the asymmetry. If the system has non-conservative debt, the designer is responsible for the holonomy. The designer cannot blame the structure on the world; the structure is their creation.

**The describer's responsibility.** The describer observes the system and is responsible for the accuracy of their description. If the describer claims that the cost is a metric when it is not, they are responsible for the error. If the describer claims that the debt is a potential difference when it is not, they are responsible for the misdescription. The describer's responsibility is to be humble and accurate.

**The relationship.** The two responsibilities are different. The designer is responsible for what they build; the describer is responsible for what they claim. The same mathematical structure can be the subject of both responsibilities: the designer builds it, the describer describes it.

**Remark 17.19.** The ethical dimension is often overlooked in mathematical theories. But it is important: mathematical structures are used to build systems and to describe the world, and both uses have consequences. The designer's choices affect the system's behavior; the describer's claims affect our understanding.

**Remark 17.20.** The complex quasi-metric is a mathematical structure with ethical implications. If it is used to model a real system, the choice of stance matters: the designer's choices and the describer's claims have different consequences. The theory does not resolve the ethical question; it clarifies it.

## 17.10 Summary

We have reflected on the nature of cost and debt.

**Two stances.**

- The **designer stance**: cost and debt are features of a system we have built. We choose the axioms; we impose the structure; the debt is our tolerance for non-metricity.
- The **describer stance**: cost and debt are features of a system we are observing. We discover the structure; we infer the axioms; the debt is a potential difference.

**The relationship.** The two stances are not contradictory; they are answers to different questions. The same mathematical structure (the complex quasi-metric) can be described from both stances, with different vocabularies.

**The vocabulary.**

- Designer: tolerance, constraint, choice.
- Describer: potential difference, axiom, discovery.

**The implications.**

- The metric assumption is legitimate from the designer stance, but not from the describer stance.
- The basepoint theorem is a consequence from the designer stance, but a discovery from the describer stance.
- Gauge invariance is a convenience from the designer stance, but a limitation from the describer stance.

**The ethical dimension.** The designer is responsible for what they build; the describer is responsible for what they claim.

The reflection is not a conclusion; it is an *opening*. The theory of the complex quasi-metric is a mathematical structure with philosophical and ethical dimensions. The two stances are a way of understanding these dimensions, and of situating the theory in a broader context.
