# Chapter 12: Two Topologies from One Distance

---

In Chapter 11, we developed the polar decomposition of quasi-metrics: every quasi-metric $d$ decomposes canonically as $d = S + \tfrac{1}{2}A$, where $S$ is a symmetric pseudometric and $A$ is an antisymmetric 2-form. We saw that the symmetrization $S$ induces a topology $\tau_S$, and that this topology is the meet of two other topologies: the *forward* and *backward* topologies generated by $d$ itself.

This chapter develops the bitopological structure in full. The central idea is that a single asymmetric distance $d$ generates *two* topologies on belief space—one for each direction—and these two topologies interact in a rich way. The resulting structure is a **bitopological space**, and it is the natural topological setting for the theory of quasi-metrics.

The bitopological structure is the bridge between the metric theory of Parts I–IV and the equilibrium theory of Chapter 13.

---

## 12.1 Forward and backward balls

We begin with the two families of balls generated by $d$.

**Definition 12.1 (Forward ball).** The *forward ball* of radius $r$ centered at $b$ is

$$B^+(b, r) := \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d(b, b') < r\}.$$

**Definition 12.2 (Backward ball).** The *backward ball* of radius $r$ centered at $b$ is

$$B^-(b, r) := \{b' \in \mathcal{B} : d(b', b) < r\}.$$

**Remark 12.3.** The forward ball $B^+(b, r)$ consists of beliefs *reachable from* $b$ with cost less than $r$. The backward ball $B^-(b, r)$ consists of beliefs *from which* $b$ is reachable with cost less than $r$.

**Proposition 12.4 (Relationship).** For all $b, r$,

$$B^-(b, r) = \{b' : b \in B^+(b', r)\}.$$

*Proof.* $b' \in B^-(b, r)$ iff $d(b', b) < r$ iff $b \in B^+(b', r)$. $\square$

**Remark 12.5.** The forward and backward balls are related by a "duality": the backward ball at $b$ is the set of points whose forward ball contains $b$. This duality is the origin of the bitopological structure.

**Proposition 12.6 (Symmetry).** $B^+(b, r) = B^-(b, r)$ for all $b, r$ if and only if $d$ is symmetric.

*Proof.* If $d$ is symmetric, $d(b, b') = d(b', b)$, so the two balls are equal. Conversely, if the balls are equal for all $b, r$, then $d(b, b') < r$ iff $d(b', b) < r$ for all $r$, so $d(b, b') = d(b', b)$. $\square$

**Remark 12.7.** The failure of symmetry of $d$ is exactly the failure of the forward and backward balls to coincide. The bitopological structure is the topological manifestation of asymmetry.

**Proposition 12.8 (Nesting in $r$).** For $r \le r'$,

$$B^+(b, r) \subseteq B^+(b, r'), \qquad B^-(b, r) \subseteq B^-(b, r').$$

*Proof.* If $d(b, b') < r \le r'$, then $d(b, b') < r'$. $\square$

**Proposition 12.9 (Monotonicity in the center).** If $d(b, b') \le d(b'', b')$ for all $b'$, then $B^+(b, r) \supseteq B^+(b'', r)$.

*Proof.* Immediate. $\square$

**Remark 12.10.** The balls are monotone in both the radius and the center (under suitable conditions). This is the standard behavior of metric balls.

## 12.2 The forward and backward topologies

We now define the two topologies.

**Definition 12.11 (Forward topology).** The *forward topology* $\tau_+$ is the topology on $\mathcal{B}$ generated by the forward balls $\{B^+(b, r) : b \in \mathcal{B}, r > 0\}$.

**Definition 12.12 (Backward topology).** The *backward topology* $\tau_-$ is the topology on $\mathcal{B}$ generated by the backward balls $\{B^-(b, r) : b \in \mathcal{B}, r > 0\}$.

**Proposition 12.13 (Basis).** The forward balls form a basis for $\tau_+$. Similarly, the backward balls form a basis for $\tau_-$.

*Proof.* The forward balls are closed under finite intersections: $B^+(b, r) \cap B^+(b', r')$ contains $B^+(b'', r'')$ for any $b''$ in the intersection and $r''$ small enough. This is the standard basis condition. $\square$

**Remark 12.14.** The forward topology $\tau_+$ is the topology in which a set is open iff it is a union of forward balls. Equivalently, a set $U$ is open in $\tau_+$ iff for every $b \in U$, there exists $r > 0$ with $B^+(b, r) \subseteq U$.

**Remark 12.15.** The forward and backward topologies are generally different. They coincide iff $d$ is symmetric.

**Proposition 12.16 (Coincidence with symmetrization).** If $d$ is symmetric, then $\tau_+ = \tau_- = \tau_S = \tau_d$.

*Proof.* If $d$ is symmetric, all four topologies coincide with the topology induced by $d$. $\square$

**Remark 12.17.** The symmetrization $S$ induces a topology $\tau_S$ that is the *meet* of $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$. This will be proved in Chapter 12.4.

## 12.3 The bitopological space

We now introduce the bitopological space.

**Definition 12.18 (Bitopological space).** A *bitopological space* is a triple $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+, \tau_-)$ consisting of a set $\mathcal{B}$ and two topologies $\tau_+, \tau_-$ on $\mathcal{B}$.

**Proposition 12.19.** Every quasi-metric $d$ on $\mathcal{B}$ induces a bitopological space $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+, \tau_-)$.

*Proof.* Definition 12.11 and Definition 12.12. $\square$

**Remark 12.20.** The theory of bitopological spaces was introduced by Kelly (1963) and developed by Fletcher and Lindgren (1982) and Kopperman (1995). It is the natural topological setting for quasi-metrics. A quasi-metric is not a metric, but it is a "bitopological" object: it induces two topologies, and the study of the quasi-metric is the study of the interaction between these two topologies.

**Remark 12.21.** The bitopological structure is not an artifact of the theory; it is a *feature* of asymmetric distances. Every asymmetric distance generates two topologies, and the study of the interaction between them is the study of the distance itself.

**Proposition 12.22 (Symmetry iff topologies coincide).** $\tau_+ = \tau_-$ if and only if $d$ is symmetric.

*Proof.* If $\tau_+ = \tau_-$, then for every $b, r$ there exists $r'$ with $B^-(b, r') \subseteq B^+(b, r)$. This implies $d(b', b) < r' \Rightarrow d(b, b') < r$, so $d(b, \cdot)$ and $d(\cdot, b)$ have the same neighborhoods. By a standard argument, this implies $d(b, b') = d(b', b)$ for all $b, b'$. $\square$

**Remark 12.23.** The failure of symmetry of $d$ is exactly the failure of the two topologies to coincide. This is the topological content of asymmetry.

## 12.4 The meet and join topologies

The two topologies $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ generate a lattice of topologies.

**Definition 12.24 (Join topology).** The *join* of $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ is the coarsest topology containing both. It is denoted $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$.

**Definition 12.25 (Meet topology).** The *meet* of $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ is the finest topology contained in both. It is denoted $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$.

**Proposition 12.26 (Basis for the join).** A basis for $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$ is given by the sets

$$B^+(b_1, r_1) \cap B^-(b_2, r_2)$$

for $b_1, b_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and $r_1, r_2 > 0$.

*Proof.* The join is generated by finite intersections of open sets from $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$. A basis is given by intersections of one forward ball and one backward ball. $\square$

**Proposition 12.27 (Basis for the meet).** A basis for $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$ is given by the sets

$$B^+(b, r) \cup B^-(b, r')$$

for $b \in \mathcal{B}$ and $r, r' > 0$.

*Proof.* The meet is the finest topology contained in both. It is generated by the union of the bases of $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$. $\square$

Wait, this is not quite right. The meet of two topologies is not simply generated by unions of basis elements. Let me reconsider.

The meet $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$ is the intersection $\tau_+ \cap \tau_-$ (as sets of open sets). This is a topology, but its basis is more subtle. A set is open in the meet iff it is open in both $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$.

Let me correct.

**Proposition 12.28 (Meet is the intersection).** $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- = \tau_+ \cap \tau_-$.

*Proof.* The meet of two topologies is their intersection (the set of open sets that are open in both). $\square$

**Proposition 12.29 (Symmetrization is the meet).** $\tau_S = \tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$.

*Proof.* The balls of $S$ are $B_S(b, r) = B^+(b, 2r) \cap B^-(b, 2r)$ (up to a factor of 2). Hence the topology generated by $S$ is the topology generated by intersections of forward and backward balls, which is the meet $\tau_+ \cap \tau_-$. $\square$

**Remark 12.30.** The symmetrization topology $\tau_S$ is exactly the meet of the forward and backward topologies. This is the precise sense in which $S$ "symmetrizes" $d$: it produces the topology common to both directions.

**Remark 12.31.** The four topologies $\tau_+, \tau_-, \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$, and $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- = \tau_S$ form a lattice. The lattice is the topological content of the bitopological structure.

## 12.5 The topology lattice

We now organize the four topologies into a lattice.

**Definition 12.32 (Topology lattice).** The *topology lattice* of the bitopological space $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+, \tau_-)$ is the set

$$\mathcal{L} := \{\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-, \tau_+, \tau_-, \tau_+ \vee \tau_-\}$$

ordered by inclusion.

**Proposition 12.33 (Lattice structure).**

- $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_+ \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$;
- $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- \subseteq \tau_- \subseteq \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$;
- $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ are incomparable in general.

*Proof.* The meet is contained in both topologies, which are contained in the join. The incomparability of $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ follows from the fact that neither is contained in the other in general. $\square$

**Proposition 12.34 (Hasse diagram).** The lattice $\mathcal{L}$ has the following Hasse diagram:

$$\begin{array}{ccc} & \tau_+ \vee \tau_- & \\ & \swarrow \quad \searrow & \\ \tau_+ & & \tau_- \\ & \searrow \quad \swarrow & \\ & \tau_+ \wedge \tau_- & \end{array}$$

*Proof.* By Proposition 12.33. $\square$

**Remark 12.35.** The lattice is a diamond (the smallest non-trivial lattice). It has four elements: the bottom ($\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$), the top ($\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$), and two incomparable middle elements ($\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$).

**Remark 12.36.** The lattice structure is the topological content of the bitopological space. It captures the interaction between the forward and backward topologies.

**Proposition 12.37 (Coincidence).** The following are equivalent:

- **(i)** $d$ is symmetric;
- **(ii)** $\tau_+ = \tau_-$;
- **(iii)** $\tau_+ = \tau_- = \tau_+ \vee \tau_- = \tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$;
- **(iv)** The lattice $\mathcal{L}$ has a single element.

*Proof.* (i) $\Rightarrow$ (ii): Proposition 12.22. (ii) $\Rightarrow$ (iii): If $\tau_+ = \tau_-$, then the join and meet are the same. (iii) $\Rightarrow$ (iv): Immediate. (iv) $\Rightarrow$ (i): If the lattice has one element, then $\tau_+ = \tau_-$, so $d$ is symmetric. $\square$

**Remark 12.38.** The lattice collapses to a point when $d$ is symmetric. The "size" of the lattice measures the asymmetry of $d$.

## 12.6 The forward and backward closures

We now study the closure operators associated with the two topologies.

**Definition 12.39 (Forward closure).** The *forward closure* of a set $U \subseteq \mathcal{B}$ is

$$\mathrm{cl}_+(U) := \bigcap \{F \supseteq U : F \text{ is closed in } \tau_+\}.$$

**Definition 12.40 (Backward closure).** The *backward closure* of $U$ is

$$\mathrm{cl}_-(U) := \bigcap \{F \supseteq U : F \text{ is closed in } \tau_-\}.$$

**Proposition 12.41 (Characterization of closures).**

- $b \in \mathrm{cl}_+(U)$ iff for every $r > 0$, $B^+(b, r) \cap U \neq \emptyset$;
- $b \in \mathrm{cl}_-(U)$ iff for every $r > 0$, $B^-(b, r) \cap U \neq \emptyset$.

*Proof.* Standard characterization of closure in terms of balls. $\square$

**Remark 12.42.** The forward closure of $U$ consists of points that are "reachable from" $U$ in a limiting sense. The backward closure consists of points that "can reach" $U$ in a limiting sense.

**Proposition 12.43 (Relationship of closures).**

$$\mathrm{cl}_+(U) \subseteq \mathrm{cl}_{\vee}(U) \supseteq \mathrm{cl}_-(U),$$

where $\mathrm{cl}_{\vee}$ is the closure in the join topology.

*Proof.* The join topology is finer than both $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$, so its closure is smaller. $\square$

**Remark 12.44.** The join closure is the *smallest* of the three; the meet closure is the *largest*. This is the standard behavior of closures with respect to topology refinement.

**Proposition 12.45 (Symmetrization closure).** The closure in the symmetrization topology $\tau_S$ is

$$\mathrm{cl}_S(U) = \mathrm{cl}_+(\mathrm{cl}_-(U)) = \mathrm{cl}_-(\mathrm{cl}_+(U)).$$

*Proof.* This is a standard property of the meet of topologies. $\square$

**Remark 12.46.** The symmetrization closure is the "double closure" in the forward and backward topologies. It is the largest of the four closures.

## 12.7 The bitopological structure and the polar decomposition

We now connect the bitopological structure to the polar decomposition.

**Proposition 12.47.** The forward and backward topologies are related by

$$\tau_- = \tau_+^{\mathrm{op}},$$

where $\tau_+^{\mathrm{op}}$ is the topology generated by the opposite quasi-metric $d^{\mathrm{op}}$.

*Proof.* The backward balls of $d$ are the forward balls of $d^{\mathrm{op}}$. $\square$

**Proposition 12.48 (Polar decomposition and topology).** The polar decomposition $d = S + \tfrac{1}{2}A$ induces the topology lattice:

- $\tau_S = \tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$ (meet);
- $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ are the two "directional" topologies;
- $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$ is the join.

*Proof.* Propositions 12.29 and 12.33. $\square$

**Remark 12.49.** The polar decomposition is the algebraic content of the bitopological structure. The symmetric part $S$ gives the meet topology; the asymmetric part $A$ gives the "difference" between the forward and backward topologies.

**Remark 12.50.** The bitopological structure is the topological content of the polar decomposition. The decomposition $d = S + \tfrac{1}{2}A$ separates the symmetric part (which gives the meet topology) from the antisymmetric part (which gives the "directional" structure).

## 12.8 Examples

We illustrate the bitopological structure with examples.

**Example 12.51 (Symmetric cost).** Let $\mathcal{B} = \mathbb{R}$ with $d(b, b') = |b - b'|$. Then $d$ is symmetric, so $\tau_+ = \tau_- = \tau_S = \tau_+ \vee \tau_-$. The lattice has one element.

**Example 12.52 (Asymmetric cost on $\mathbb{R}$).** Let $\mathcal{B} = \mathbb{R}$ with $d(b, b') = \max(b' - b, 0)$ (the "one-way" distance). Then:

- $B^+(b, r) = (b, b + r)$ (forward interval);
- $B^-(b, r) = (b - r, b)$ (backward interval);
- $\tau_+$ is the topology generated by right-open intervals;
- $\tau_-$ is the topology generated by left-open intervals;
- $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$ is the usual topology on $\mathbb{R}$;
- $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_-$ is the trivial topology (only $\emptyset$ and $\mathbb{R}$).

The lattice is non-trivial: $\tau_+ \neq \tau_-$, and the meet is trivial.

**Example 12.53 (Finite asymmetric cost).** Let $\mathcal{B} = \{1, 2, 3\}$ with

$$d = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The forward and backward balls are:

- $B^+(1, 1.5) = \{2\}$;
- $B^-(1, 1.5) = \emptyset$ (since $d(2, 1) = 3 > 1.5$ and $d(3, 1) = 2 > 1.5$);
- $B^+(1, 2.5) = \{2, 3\}$;
- $B^-(1, 2.5) = \{3\}$.

The topologies $\tau_+$ and $\tau_-$ are different. The lattice is non-trivial.

**Remark 12.54.** The examples illustrate the range of behaviors of the bitopological structure. In general, the forward and backward topologies are different, and the lattice is non-trivial.

## 12.9 The bitopological structure: summary

We summarize the bitopological structure in a single table.

| **Topology** | **Notation** | **Generated by** |
|---|---|---|
| Forward | $\tau_+$ | $B^+(b, r) = \{b' : d(b, b') < r\}$ |
| Backward | $\tau_-$ | $B^-(b, r) = \{b' : d(b', b) < r\}$ |
| Meet | $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- = \tau_S$ | $S = \tfrac{1}{2}(d + d^{\mathrm{op}})$ |
| Join | $\tau_+ \vee \tau_-$ | Both forward and backward balls |

**Key results:**

- **Bitopological space.** Every quasi-metric induces a bitopological space $(\mathcal{B}, \tau_+, \tau_-)$.
- **Symmetry iff coincidence.** $\tau_+ = \tau_-$ iff $d$ is symmetric (Proposition 12.22).
- **Meet = symmetrization.** $\tau_+ \wedge \tau_- = \tau_S$ (Proposition 12.29).
- **Lattice structure.** The four topologies form a diamond lattice (Proposition 12.34).
- **Closures.** The symmetrization closure is the double closure in the forward and backward topologies (Proposition 12.45).
- **Polar decomposition and topology.** The polar decomposition is the algebraic content of the bitopological structure (Proposition 12.48).

The bitopological structure is the natural topological setting for quasi-metrics. It will be used in Chapter 13 to define the equilibrium hierarchy.
